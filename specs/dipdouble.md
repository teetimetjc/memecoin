# Does a fallen favourite come back?

Frozen 2026-10-09, before any of the data below was looked at.

## The claim being tested

When a heavy favourite — a contract trading at 90–96¢ nine minutes before
close — falls hard during the window, does it recover more often than a
generic fall does?

This matters because of a number that is almost certainly a mirage. Across
the 45 live bets placed so far, 14 fell below 50¢ at some point and **11 of
those 14 recovered to settle YES — 78.6%**. Buying more at 50¢ on each would
have returned about +$32 on $56 staked.

Fourteen observations. The Wilson interval runs from roughly 52% to 93%, so
it barely clears the 50% break-even at the bottom. And this project has
already tested dip-buying at scale: `dip.py` found that **56% of 10-point
falls never came back**, and the blunt version of that trade lost $2.21 a
bet.

So there are two answers, 79% and 44%, and the one resting on fourteen
observations is the one to distrust. This test asks the larger sample.

## Why it could nonetheless be true

There is a real mechanism, which is why this is worth an afternoon rather
than a dismissal. A contract at 93¢ that drops to 45¢ has moved an enormous
distance in a few minutes. For a 15-minute crypto market that move is more
likely to be noise — a thin book, one large order, a brief spike in the
underlying — than news, because there is rarely any news inside fifteen
minutes. A generic 10-point fall from 60¢ carries no such implication.

If that is right, the favourite's dip should recover more often than the
generic dip, in the same data, over the same period. That is a comparison,
not a single number, and the test is built as one.

## The rule, exactly

**Leg 1 (the favourite).** At T−9, `ask9` in [0.90, 0.96). This is the live
narrow-slice rule, unchanged.

**The dip.** At T−6 or T−3, the YES **bid** at that offset is at or below
**50¢**. The bid, not the mid or the ask: the bid is the real, observable
collapse. The first offset at which this is true is the trigger.

**Leg 2 (the double-down).** Buy $4 more at the **ask at that same offset** —
the price you would actually pay, at a moment you could actually have acted,
knowing only what was visible then. Hold to settlement.

T−1 is **not** an eligible trigger. One minute is not enough time to
rebound, and the offset nearest settlement is where any leakage of the
answer into the price would live.

**Stake:** $4, whole contracts, Kalshi's fee `ceil(0.07 × n × P × (1−P))`
cents applied to each leg.

## The control arm, which is the actual test

The same dip rule applied to markets that were **not** favourites at T−9 —
`ask9` below 0.90. Same data, same period, same offsets, same arithmetic.

A high recovery rate in the favourite arm means nothing on its own, because
a market that dipped and recovered may simply be what these markets do. The
claim is specifically that **favourites recover more often than non-
favourites**, and only the gap between the two arms tests it.

## Pass conditions — all six, fixed now

1. At least **300** dip events in the favourite arm.
2. Recovery rate in the favourite arm **above 60%**.
3. Net profit on leg 2 alone **above zero**, after fees.
4. Clustered t on leg 2 **above 2.5**, clustered by close-time.
5. The favourite arm's recovery rate exceeds the control arm's by at least
   **10 percentage points**.
6. Still passes 2–5 after deleting the two best and two worst close-times.

Anything less is a fail. The t-bar is 2.5 rather than the usual 2.0 because
this hypothesis was generated by looking at a number, not reasoned from a
mechanism first.

## What a pass would and would not license

A pass would make this worth a forward paper test. It would **not** license
betting it, for three reasons that stand regardless of the result:

- **Doubling down is a second order on one market.** The live bot was just
  changed to forbid exactly that, after two orders in one ETH window lost
  $6.83 together. This rule reintroduces it deliberately, so the position
  sizing has to be argued separately and has not been.
- **You cannot buy the bottom.** This test buys at the ask at a fixed
  offset, which is honest, but a live version would need a resting order and
  that is a different trade with a different fill distribution.
- **Both sides of these markets lose.** The round trip costs about 25% of
  stake. A recovery rate above break-even is not the same as an edge once
  that is paid.
